\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Object detectors deployed on embedded and edge platforms operate under strict compute, latency, and energy constraints. Traditional detectors, however, are restricted to a single operating point, applying uniform computation to every frame regardless of scene difficulty or the optimal accuracy-efficiency trade-off. In practice, this trade-off varies substantially across inputs. For example, in autonomous driving, complex urban scenes containing dense traffic, occlusions, and numerous objects benefit from deeper processing to maximize detection accuracy. In contrast, highway scenes are often less cluttered and place a greater emphasis on low latency and high throughput, making shallower inference preferable. This input-dependent variation motivates the need for dynamic inference.


Recent advances, such as the AnyDepth-YOLO~\cite{kang2026anydepth}, enable this dynamic inference by embedding multiple execution depths within a single network. 
It exposes a \emph{super} configuration (higher accuracy and computation) and a \emph{base} configuration (lower computation and modest accuracy degradation). 
While this architecture provides the capacity for input-adaptive trade-offs, realizing these benefits at deployment requires a highly efficient \emph{router} to determine, on a per-input basis, whether the additional computation of the super path is justified.


The central difficulty, however, lies in training this router. 
The dominant approach for learned routing trains a discrete policy using an efficiency-aware objective that linearly combines an accuracy term and a computation term, frequently incorporating an extra uniformity or entropy term. 
This formulation introduces three primary complications. 
First, the accuracy and efficiency terms are in direct conflict, and their balance is governed by a trade-off weight that \emph{bakes a single operating point into the trained routing policy}; consequently, serving a new compute budget under this formulation requires retraining the router. 
Second, because the routing decision is discrete and sampled (e.g., via Gumbel-Softmax or policy gradients), training is prone to collapsing onto a single path, which typically requires counteraction through auxiliary terms and their associated hyperparameters. 
Third, the sampled estimator injects high variance, which complicates optimization and can obscure which inputs truly warrant the deeper path.


We reframe depth routing as \emph{an advantage regression} problem. 
Because the detector itself remains frozen, the benefit of selecting the deeper path for a given image is a fixed quantity: the per-image super advantage, defined as the reduction in detection loss achieved by the deep path relative to the shallow path.
Instead of learning a discrete routing policy, a lightweight prediction head directly regress this advantage.
At inference time, routing reduces to comparing the predicted advantage against a threshold. 
This decouples training from the deployment operating point: a single trained router produces a continuous accuracy--efficiency Pareto curve through a simple threshold sweep,
with no trade-off weight and no collapse-prevention term. 


On KITTI, trained on
detection images and evaluated on tracking videos, the proposed router
Pareto-dominates random, luminance, edge-density, and confidence-based routing
baselines, and the result transfers to BDD100K. The router adds negligible
overhead, well under a thousandth of a percent of the backbone computation,
while yielding measurable on-device latency, energy, and frame-rate gains; for
instance, at a sweet-spot threshold it matches the deep path's accuracy while
reducing per-frame computation by about one sixth.
